Ethical Dilemma

Insider misuse of administrative access

Analysis

The dilemma I analyzed involves Reyes Daniel Ruiz, a reliability engineer who worked at Yahoo for over ten years supporting Yahoo Mail. Ruiz used the administrative access his job gave him to crack passwords and break into about 6,000 Yahoo accounts, most belonging to younger women, including friends and coworkers. He used password resets to also access linked accounts on other platforms, including iCloud, Facebook, Gmail, and Dropbox, and copied private images and videos to a personal hard drive at home.

Ruiz clearly violated both professional and ethical rules. His job gave him access to internal systems for legitimate maintenance, not personal use. Using that access to target specific people for personal reasons is a direct abuse of trust, regardless of whether any law was technically broken. It's also a clear violation of the principle of least privilege, which says access should only be used for its intended purpose.

A coworker noticed the suspicious activity and reported it in June 2018. Once caught, Ruiz destroyed the hard drive and resigned that same month. He was later arrested, pleaded guilty in September 2019, and was sentenced to five years of probation and home confinement, along with financial restitution.

A Better Alternative

The ethical principles I believe are essential here are honesty, respect for privacy, and accountability. Ruiz's actions violated all three, since he used access meant for maintaining systems to instead invade the privacy of people he knew personally, and only stopped once he realized he'd been caught rather than on his own.

A better alternative would have involved two changes, one from Ruiz and one from Yahoo. Ruiz should have simply never used his access outside of his actual job duties. If he had concerns about security weaknesses, the honest path would have been to report them through an internal channel, not exploit them personally. On Yahoo's side, the company could have prevented this by requiring a documented business reason for any employee accessing a specific user's account, logging every access attempt, and running regular audits to catch unusual patterns early instead of relying on a coworker to notice and report it.

This alternative is better because it protects everyone involved. Users would have kept their privacy regardless of one employee's curiosity. Ruiz would have kept his job and his freedom. And Yahoo would have avoided the legal and reputational damage that comes from a case like this becoming public. Strong access controls don't just punish bad behavior after the fact, they make it harder for the behavior to happen in the first place.
